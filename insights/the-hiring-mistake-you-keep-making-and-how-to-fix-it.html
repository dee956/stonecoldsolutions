The Hiring Mistake You Keep Making (And How to Fix It)

Hiring is one of the most consequential things a business does, yet most organizations treat it as a reactive exercise — scrambling to fill a vacancy rather than building a deliberate system. The result is a revolving door that costs money, drains team morale, and stalls growth. Fixing it doesn't require an HR department of twenty people. It requires clearer thinking, a few structural changes, and consistent follow-through.

Write Job Descriptions That Reflect Reality

Most job postings are wish lists dressed up as requirements. They pile on credentials, years of experience, and a laundry list of responsibilities that would challenge a small army. Candidates either self-select out unnecessarily or, worse, accept the role and discover the job bears little resemblance to what was advertised.

Before you post a role, answer two questions honestly: What does success look like in the first 90 days? And what would genuinely disqualify someone from doing this job well? Build the posting around those answers. A tighter, more honest description attracts fewer applicants — and far more of the right ones.

Structure Your Interviews So They Actually Reveal Something

Unstructured interviews feel natural but produce unreliable results. When each interviewer asks different questions and scores candidates on gut instinct, you're not gathering comparable data — you're holding a series of pleasant conversations and hoping something useful surfaces.

A structured approach doesn't mean robotic. It means every candidate for a given role is asked the same core questions, evaluated against the same criteria, and scored before interviewers discuss their impressions with each other. This reduces groupthink and makes it far easier to compare candidates fairly. Behavioral questions — "Tell me about a time when…" — tend to be more predictive than hypotheticals, because past behavior in real situations reveals more than polished answers to imaginary ones.

Onboarding Is Not Orientation

Many companies conflate onboarding with the first day: a tour, some paperwork, and access to the right systems. That's orientation. Onboarding is the deliberate process of helping a new employee become effective — and it should run for at least 60 to 90 days.

A new hire who feels confused, unsupported, or invisible in their first month doesn't become fully engaged later. They start quietly looking for the exit.

Effective onboarding includes clear 30-, 60-, and 90-day milestones; a named point of contact beyond the direct manager; and structured check-ins that create space for honest feedback in both directions. The investment is modest. The payoff — in speed-to-productivity and early retention — is significant.

Culture Is Built in the Small Moments, Not the Big Statements

Workplace culture isn't what's written in a values document. It's what happens in a team meeting when someone pushes back on a bad idea. It's how a manager responds when an employee makes an honest mistake. It's whether people feel safe raising a concern or whether they've learned — through observation — that doing so carries a penalty.

If you want to understand your actual culture, pay less attention to surveys and more to behavior: who gets promoted, whose ideas get credited, how conflict is handled, and what gets tolerated. These patterns, repeated over time, are the culture — regardless of what any poster on the wall says.

Retention Starts Before Someone Decides to Leave

Most retention conversations happen too late — when a valued employee has already accepted another offer. By that point, a counter-offer is rarely the answer, and the underlying reason for leaving hasn't changed.

Proactive retention looks like this:

  • Regular one-on-one conversations focused on career development, not just task status
  • Clear, transparent criteria for advancement so people know what "getting ahead" actually looks like at your company
  • Consistent recognition of contributions — specific and timely, not generic and annual
  • Honest conversations about workload before burnout becomes resignation

None of this requires a large budget. It requires managers who are paying attention and an organization that makes it structurally possible for them to do so.

Hiring and retention aren't separate problems — they're two halves of the same system. When the hiring process is honest and deliberate, when onboarding is treated as a genuine investment, and when culture is managed through behavior rather than slogans, the gap between the people you attract and the people you keep gets dramatically smaller. That's not a talent strategy. That's just good management, done consistently.
