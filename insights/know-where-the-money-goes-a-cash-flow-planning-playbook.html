Know Where the Money Goes: A Cash Flow Planning Playbook

Many businesses that fail are technically profitable when they close their doors. That is not a paradox — it is a cash flow problem. Revenue recognition, invoice timing, inventory purchases, and loan repayments can create a gap between what your income statement says and what is actually sitting in your account on the 15th of the month. Closing that gap requires deliberate planning, not optimism. Here is how to do it.

Understand the Difference Between Cash and Profit

Profit is an accounting concept. Cash is what pays your suppliers, your staff, and your rent. The two figures diverge the moment you extend credit to customers, carry inventory, prepay expenses, or take on debt. A business can book strong revenue in March, collect none of it until May, and still have to meet payroll in April. Until your leadership team internalizes this distinction, every financial discussion in your organization is built on a shaky foundation. Start there.

Build a Rolling 13-Week Cash Flow Forecast

A 13-week horizon — roughly one quarter — is long enough to anticipate problems and short enough to stay accurate. The structure is straightforward: list every expected cash inflow (customer payments, loan proceeds, asset sales) and every expected outflow (payroll, rent, supplier invoices, tax installments, debt service) week by week. The net figure for each week, carried forward as a running balance, tells you exactly when you are likely to be tight.

The goal is not to predict the future perfectly. The goal is to see trouble coming with enough lead time to do something about it.

Update the forecast every week. Replace estimates with actuals as they come in, and push the horizon forward so you always have 13 weeks of visibility. This discipline alone separates businesses that manage cash from businesses that react to crises.

Accelerate Inflows and Delay Outflows — Strategically

Once you have a forecast, your lever is timing. On the inflow side, consider:

  • Shortening payment terms. If you currently offer net-60, test whether key customers will accept net-30. Many will, especially if you ask rather than assume.
  • Invoicing immediately. Every day between delivery and invoice is a day of unnecessary delay. Automate or systematize invoice generation so it happens the same day work is completed or goods are shipped.
  • Offering early-payment incentives. A small discount for prompt payment can be worth far more than the cost if it reliably tightens your collection cycle.

On the outflow side, negotiate payment terms with suppliers the same way your customers negotiate with you. Extending from net-30 to net-45 or net-60 with a key vendor is free working capital. Use it. The important caveat: delay outflows strategically, not by simply ignoring due dates. Damaged supplier relationships and late fees erode the gains quickly.

Maintain a Cash Reserve — and Define What It Covers

A reserve is not an emergency fund sitting idle; it is an operational buffer that keeps you from making bad decisions under pressure. Defining the reserve in concrete terms matters. Rather than a vague goal of "three months of expenses," calculate the specific dollar figure that covers your fixed obligations — payroll, rent, essential subscriptions, minimum debt payments — for a defined period. That number becomes a floor. When your cash balance approaches it, the response protocol activates: defer discretionary spending, accelerate collections, draw on a credit facility if one exists.

If you do not yet have a revolving credit facility, establish one before you need it. Banks extend credit readily to businesses that do not appear desperate. Waiting until a cash crisis arrives to apply is the worst possible timing.

Align Financial Planning With Your Operating Calendar

Most businesses have predictable cash patterns tied to their operating cycle — seasonal demand, quarterly tax payments, annual contract renewals, inventory build-ups before peak periods. Map these patterns explicitly into your annual financial plan. If you know that October and November are heavy outflow months due to inventory purchasing, the plan should show how you fund that buildup, whether through retained cash, a credit draw, or adjusted supplier terms. Surprises should be genuinely unforeseeable events, not recurring features of your own business cycle.

Cash flow planning is not a finance-department task that surfaces at month-end. It is an ongoing management discipline that belongs at the center of how you run the business. Build the forecast, work the levers, protect the floor, and plan around your own operating reality — and you will have the financial footing to make decisions from a position of strength rather than necessity.
