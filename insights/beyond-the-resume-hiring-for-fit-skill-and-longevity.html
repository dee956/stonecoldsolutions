Beyond the Resume: Hiring for Fit, Skill, and Longevity

Most hiring mistakes don't happen because a candidate lied on their resume. They happen because the hiring process was designed to screen for the wrong things—credentials over capability, polish over potential, availability over alignment. For growing companies especially, a bad hire isn't just an inconvenience; it's a drain on time, morale, and money that compounds quickly. Building a better process doesn't require a full HR department. It requires clarity and discipline at each stage.

Define the Role Before You Write the Job Posting

The most overlooked step in hiring is the one that happens before the job posting goes live. Too many companies copy a previous listing, borrow language from a competitor's ad, or write a wish list of qualifications that has little to do with day-to-day reality. Before you write a single word, answer these questions internally: What does success in this role look like after 90 days? After one year? What decisions will this person make independently? Who will they need to collaborate with most? What skills are genuinely required versus merely preferred?

A job posting built from those answers will attract candidates who understand what they're walking into—and will self-select out if they don't fit. That saves everyone time.

Structure Your Interviews Around Real Work

Unstructured interviews—where each candidate gets different questions based on wherever the conversation drifts—are one of the least reliable ways to evaluate people. They tend to favor candidates who are confident and well-rehearsed, not necessarily those who are most capable.

A more useful approach combines a small number of consistent behavioral questions with a practical task that mirrors actual work. For a project manager role, that might mean reviewing a brief and identifying risks. For a customer-facing role, it might mean handling a simulated escalation. The task doesn't need to be elaborate—it needs to be relevant. What you're looking for is how the candidate thinks, not just what they say.

The goal of a good interview isn't to make a candidate perform under pressure. It's to give them a realistic preview of the role and give yourself an honest look at how they work.

Evaluate Culture Add, Not Culture Fit

"Culture fit" has become a way to describe hiring people who are similar to whoever is already in the room. That leads to homogeneous teams that share the same blind spots and struggle to adapt. A more productive frame is culture add: does this person share the values that matter most to how your organization operates, while bringing a perspective or approach that isn't already well-represented?

To make this assessment concrete, you need to articulate your actual values—not the aspirational ones on your website, but the ones that show up in how decisions get made, how conflict is handled, and what behaviors get rewarded. If you haven't done that work, "culture fit" will always default to gut feeling, and gut feeling tends to favor familiarity.

Onboarding Is Part of the Hire

Many companies spend significant effort recruiting a candidate and then leave them to figure things out on their own in the first few weeks. The result is a slow ramp-up at best and early attrition at worst. Onboarding isn't orientation paperwork and a laptop setup—it's a structured process of helping a new employee build relationships, understand expectations, and make their first meaningful contributions.

A practical onboarding plan should include:

  • A clear 30-60-90 day framework with specific milestones
  • Scheduled check-ins with their manager in weeks one, two, and four
  • Introductions to key stakeholders—not just their immediate team
  • Early, low-stakes opportunities to contribute and get feedback

New employees decide quickly whether they made the right choice. A deliberate onboarding process signals that the organization takes that question seriously.

Retention Starts at the Offer Stage

Hiring is expensive—in time, in fees, in the productivity lost during a vacancy. Yet many companies invest heavily in recruiting and almost nothing in retention. Compensation matters, but it's rarely the first reason people leave. More often, they leave because expectations weren't clear, growth wasn't possible, or their manager wasn't equipped to support them.

The conversations that prevent turnover—about career development, workload, recognition, and direction—should happen regularly, not only at annual reviews. Building those touchpoints into how managers operate is less about adding process and more about making good management the default.

Hiring well and retaining the people you hire are two sides of the same discipline. When your process is clear, honest, and consistent from job posting to first anniversary, you spend less time backfilling roles and more time building the team your business actually needs.
