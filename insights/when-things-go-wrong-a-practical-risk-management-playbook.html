When Things Go Wrong: A Practical Risk Management Playbook

No business operates in a risk-free environment. Supply chains break, key employees leave, cybersecurity incidents happen, markets shift, and occasionally the world throws something genuinely unprecedented at everyone at once. The companies that navigate these moments well are rarely the ones that got lucky—they are the ones that did the unglamorous work of preparation before pressure arrived. Risk management is not a compliance exercise. Done right, it is a competitive advantage.

Start With an Honest Risk Inventory

Most organizations underestimate the range of risks they face because they only think about the ones that have already hurt them. A useful risk inventory goes broader than that. Divide your exposure into categories: operational (process failures, key-person dependencies, technology outages), financial (cash flow gaps, customer concentration, currency exposure), reputational (social media, regulatory scrutiny, partner conduct), and external (economic shifts, supply chain disruptions, natural events).

For each risk, assess two dimensions independently: likelihood and impact. A low-likelihood, high-impact event—say, losing your largest customer—deserves more attention than a frequent but easily absorbed nuisance. This simple matrix exercise, done with your leadership team, will surface blind spots that no individual alone would catch. Revisit it at least annually, and whenever your business model changes significantly.

Reduce Concentration Before It Becomes a Crisis

Concentration risk is one of the most common and avoidable threats in business. It shows up in several forms:

  • Customer concentration: A single client representing more than 20–25% of revenue creates dangerous dependency.
  • Supplier concentration: Relying on one vendor for a critical input leaves you exposed if they face their own disruption.
  • People concentration: If one person holds critical institutional knowledge or relationships, their departure can cripple an operation.
  • Geographic concentration: Facilities, teams, or markets clustered in one location multiply the impact of any regional event.

Reducing concentration takes deliberate effort and often short-term cost. Qualifying a second supplier, cross-training staff, or diversifying your customer base requires investment today for protection tomorrow. Businesses that make these moves proactively are in a fundamentally different position when disruption hits.

Build Financial Buffers That Actually Work

Resilience is, in large part, a financial question. Companies that survive crises almost always have one thing in common: access to liquidity when they need it most. That means maintaining a cash reserve—typically three to six months of operating expenses for an SME—and securing a line of credit before you need it, not during a crisis when lenders tighten standards.

Review your fixed versus variable cost structure honestly. A business carrying heavy fixed overheads has limited room to maneuver when revenue drops. Where possible, build in flexibility: variable staffing arrangements, shorter lease terms, or scalable technology contracts. The goal is not to eliminate fixed costs—some are unavoidable and necessary—but to know exactly where you can flex and how quickly.

Write the Crisis Plan Nobody Wants to Write

A crisis plan does not need to be a lengthy document. It needs to be specific, accessible, and actually used. For each significant risk scenario, document three things: who takes charge, what the first 24–48 hours look like, and how you communicate—internally and externally.

The first hour of a crisis is almost always managed better by preparation than by intelligence. Decisions made under panic rarely hold up; decisions made in advance almost always do.

Run a tabletop exercise once a year. Gather your leadership team, present a realistic scenario—a data breach, a key facility going offline, a sudden loss of a major client—and walk through your response. You will find gaps. That is the point. Finding them in a meeting room is far less costly than finding them under real pressure.

Embed Risk Thinking Into Day-to-Day Operations

The most resilient businesses do not treat risk as a separate department or an annual review. They build it into how decisions are made. Before launching a new product, entering a new market, or signing a major contract, ask: what could go wrong, and what is our response if it does? This does not mean paralysis or excessive caution—it means making decisions with open eyes.

Assign clear ownership for risk monitoring across your leadership team. No single person can track everything, but every significant risk should have someone responsible for watching it. Make risk a standing agenda item in leadership meetings, not an afterthought.

Disruption is not a question of if—it is a question of when and how well-prepared you are to meet it. The businesses that emerge stronger from hard periods are not the ones with the most resources; they are the ones that took risk management seriously when times were good. Start that work now, while you still have the luxury of time.
