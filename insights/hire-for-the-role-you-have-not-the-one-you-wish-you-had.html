Hire for the Role You Have, Not the One You Wish You Had

Hiring is one of the highest-leverage decisions a business makes, and one of the most frequently rushed. Under pressure to fill a seat, managers write vague job descriptions, conduct unstructured interviews, and end up choosing whoever seemed most confident in the room. The result is a mismatch that costs time, money, and morale to unwind. Getting hiring right doesn't require a large HR department — it requires a clear process applied consistently, every time.

Start With the Role, Not the Person

Before you write a single line of a job posting, spend thirty minutes answering three questions: What specific outcomes does this role need to produce in its first six months? What are the two or three non-negotiable skills or capabilities required to achieve those outcomes? And what does success actually look like at the twelve-month mark?

This exercise forces you to hire for the role you genuinely have open — not an idealized version of it, and not a recycled description from a previous hire. When the outcomes are clear, the job posting becomes sharper, the interview questions become more relevant, and the evaluation criteria become measurable rather than impressionistic.

Write Job Descriptions That Attract, Not Just Inform

Most job descriptions read like legal disclaimers. They list requirements exhaustively but say almost nothing about why a talented person would want the role. A strong posting does two things simultaneously: it disqualifies candidates who are a poor fit, and it genuinely interests candidates who are a strong one.

To do that, include a brief, honest description of the team and working environment, the specific challenges the person will be solving, and what growth or learning the role realistically offers. Avoid inflating the excitement of routine work — candidates who join based on overselling leave faster and with more frustration.

Structure the Interview to Reduce Bias

Unstructured interviews — where each interviewer asks different questions and scores candidates on gut feeling — are among the weakest predictors of job performance. Structured interviews, where every candidate answers the same core questions and responses are scored against a defined rubric, produce far more reliable results.

A practical approach is to build each interview around three to five behavioral questions tied directly to the role's key outcomes. Behavioral questions ask candidates to describe specific past situations rather than hypothetical ones. For example, instead of "How would you handle a difficult client?", ask "Tell me about a time you had to manage a client who was dissatisfied with your work — what happened and what did you do?" Past behavior, while not a perfect signal, is a more reliable one than hypothetical answers.

Don't Skip the Reference Check

Reference checks are routinely treated as a formality — a box to tick after the decision has already been made. That's a mistake. A well-conducted reference call, where you ask the same structured questions you used in the interview and probe for specifics, can surface patterns that didn't emerge in the interview process.

The best reference question isn't "Would you rehire this person?" — it's "What kind of environment does this person thrive in, and what kind frustrates them?" The answers reveal fit in ways that a candidate's self-presentation rarely does.

If a reference is evasive or gives only surface-level answers, that's a data point too. Push gently for concrete examples, and pay attention to what isn't being said.

Onboarding Is Part of the Hire

A new employee's first thirty to sixty days determine whether your investment in hiring pays off or quietly erodes. Yet many businesses treat onboarding as a half-day admin exercise. Consider structuring the first month around three priorities:

  • Clarity: Make sure the new hire understands exactly what they're accountable for and how success will be measured.
  • Connection: Introduce them to the people, tools, and context they need to do the job — don't assume they'll find these on their own.
  • Early wins: Identify one or two meaningful tasks they can complete in the first two weeks to build confidence and momentum.

A structured onboarding process doesn't need to be elaborate. It needs to be intentional.

Closing Thoughts

Great hiring is a skill that improves with deliberate practice. The businesses that consistently bring in strong people aren't lucky — they've built a repeatable process, they reflect honestly on the hires that didn't work out, and they treat each stage of hiring as worthy of as much rigor as any other business function. Start with one part of your current process and make it more structured. The compounding effect over time is significant.
